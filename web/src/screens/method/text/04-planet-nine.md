# The search for Planet Nine

Since 2014 astronomers have argued that the orbits of the most distant known objects in the solar system point to an unseen planet, now called Planet Nine, a few times as massive as Earth, hundreds of astronomical units from the Sun. It has not been found. The searches have excluded most of the places where it was expected to be bright, and what remains is faint and slow, which is the kind of body we look for.

## Why we think it may exist

In 2014 Chadwick Trujillo and Scott Sheppard reported 2012 VP113, an object like Sedna whose orbit never comes closer to the Sun than 80 au, and noted that a few such objects have orbits that point the same way, which a distant massive planet could explain. In 2016 Konstantin Batygin and Michael Brown showed that the clustering of the orbits in direction and in plane has a small chance of being random and is explained by a planet of about 10 Earth masses on a distant, tilted, eccentric orbit. The same planet explains objects with orbits strongly tilted to the planets' plane.

Their later work (Batygin, Adams, Brown and Becker 2019) gives a mass of 5 to 10 Earth masses, an inclination of 15 to 25 degrees and a semi-major axis of 400 to 800 au. The most recent fit (Brown and Batygin 2021) is a mass of 6.2 Earth masses, a semi-major axis of about 380 au (+140, −80), an inclination of 16 ± 5 degrees and a perihelion near 300 au, with the sky position most likely within 12 degrees of declination of an orbit of that inclination.

Not everyone agrees that the clustering is real: surveys such as OSSOS found strong observational biases in the discovered objects, and Napier et al. 2021 found that 14 such objects are consistent with a uniform distribution of directions.

Sources: Trujillo & Sheppard 2014, Nature 507, 471; Batygin & Brown 2016, AJ 151, 22 ([arXiv:1601.05438](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.05438)); Batygin et al. 2019 ([arXiv:1902.10103](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10103)); Brown & Batygin 2021 ([arXiv:2108.09868](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09868)); Shankman et al. 2017 ([arXiv:1706.05348](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.05348)); Napier et al. 2021 ([arXiv:2102.05601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05601)).

## How it has been searched for

- **Infrared, WISE and NEOWISE.** Meisner et al. searched more than three quarters of the sky in time-resolved WISE images at 3.4 µm for a slowly moving source, reaching W1 of about 16.7 at high Galactic latitude, and found none ([arXiv:1712.04950](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04950)). The volunteers of the Backyard Worlds project blink the same kind of images.
- **Optical surveys.** Brown and Batygin searched the Zwicky Transient Facility with 95 % efficiency at V of about 20.5 and ruled out 56 % of the predicted region; the Dark Energy Survey removed about 5 % more; Pan-STARRS1, complete at half level to V of 21.5, brought the total to 78 % ([arXiv:2110.13117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.13117), [arXiv:2203.07642](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07642), [arXiv:2401.17977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17977)). What remains lies fainter than V 21 and near where the Galactic plane crosses the ecliptic.
- **Spacecraft ranging.** Cassini's range measurements exclude parts of the orbit for a 10 Earth-mass planet at 700 au and prefer others (Fienga et al. 2016, [arXiv:1602.06116](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.06116); Holman & Payne 2016, [arXiv:1604.03180](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.03180)).
- **Far infrared, IRAS and AKARI.** In 2025 Phan, Goto and colleagues compared the all-sky maps of IRAS (1983) and AKARI (2006), 23 years apart, for sources that moved by about 3 arcminutes a year; of the pairs that survived the selection one candidate remained after inspection, and they state that two detections cannot give the orbit ([arXiv:2504.17288](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17288)). A second AKARI search by Chen, Goto and colleagues reports two candidates ([arXiv:2506.12854](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12854)).

## Why it is still not found

A planet of a few Earth masses at 400 to 800 au reflects very little sunlight and is faint, and its emission is thermal and cold. It moves slowly: Earth's own orbit makes a distant body trace a loop of several arcminutes a year on the sky, with its true motion a small part of that. Brown and Batygin 2021 note that the brightest cases would already have been detected, the faintest need 8 to 10 metre telescopes, and a small dark planet could be about 3 magnitudes fainter than an average one. The Vera Rubin Observatory's single images are expected to reach r of about 24.3, which covers much of the remaining range.

## What SPHEREx adds

SPHEREx observes at 0.75 to 5 µm and sees every place several times a year from different positions of the Earth's orbit, so a distant body shifts by a large, known parallax between passes. Our search for bodies beyond Neptune adds the exposures of a pass along the motion a body at each trial distance would have; at the right distance a point comes into focus. The depth of each search is its measured noise limit, stated with its result, and we claim no Planet Nine.
