# How Pluto was found

In 1930 Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto by looking at the same patch of sky on two photographic plates taken days apart and switching quickly between them: every star stayed in place and one point of light jumped. Our blink does the same thing on SPHEREx images. The only difference is that we first have to make the background stand still, because SPHEREx exposures do not look alike the way two photographic plates of one telescope do.

## The search for Planet X

Percival Lowell and William Pickering predicted a planet beyond Neptune from irregularities in the orbit of Uranus. Lowell called it Planet X and searched for it at his observatory in Arizona from 1905 to 1916. Years later the observatory took up the search again, and Clyde Tombaugh photographed the sky systematically, plate pair after plate pair.

Sources: [Lowell Observatory exhibit](https://collectionslowellobservatory.omeka.net/exhibits/show/pluto/the-discovery); [Astronomy magazine](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/feb-18-1930-discovery-of-pluto/).

## The blink comparator

Tombaugh took plates in pairs, several days apart, with a 13-inch (33 cm) astrograph; each plate is 14 by 17 inches. A blink comparator, built by Zeiss, shows one plate to the eye and then the other, switching several times a second through two eyepieces and a mirror. Stars are identical on both plates and look still. A body that moved between the exposures changes place and appears to jump back and forth. Tombaugh looked for a mover slower than an asteroid, because a planet at the distance of Neptune creeps across the sky.

Sources: [Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/finding-pluto-blink-comparator); [APS News, March 2009](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2009/03/tombaugh-discovery-pluto-1930).

## The discovery

Tombaugh photographed the region of Delta Geminorum on 21, 23 and 29 January 1930. On 18 February 1930, comparing the plates of 23 and 29 January, he saw a point that had moved by about 3 mm on the plate. The observatory telegraphed Harvard on 12 March and announced the planet on 13 March 1930, Lowell's birthday. Pluto was then about 15th magnitude.

Pluto turned out to be about 0.002 Earth masses, far too small to disturb Neptune and Uranus as Lowell had calculated; later better measurements of Neptune's mass removed the supposed irregularities. The planet was found by a systematic search, not by the prediction.

Sources: Lowell exhibit and APS News above; Pluto's mass from [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/dwarf-planets/pluto/facts/) (about one sixth of the Moon's mass).

## Our blink

The idea carries over directly. A blink needs two things: a stable background and a change of position over a known time. We build the first and show the second at three time scales.

- **Years.** For each pass we make one clean picture of the sky and blink the passes in the order 1, 2, 3, 2. A star with proper motion swings back and forth while everything else stands still. Barnard's Star moves by about 10 arcseconds a year.
- **Days.** Within a pass we make one picture per visit and blink the visits. An asteroid hops from visit to visit.
- **Hours.** Within a visit each exposure is a frame, which catches the fastest movers.
